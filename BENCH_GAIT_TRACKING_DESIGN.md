# Bench gait-tracking experiment — design and model choice

**Status: DESIGN ONLY. No code written, no model modified, nothing run.**
Written 2026-09-03. Every number tagged **[MEASURED]** was read directly out of the model
files named below. Numbers tagged **[COMPUTED]** are derived analytically from those authored
values (parallel-axis on the MJCF `<inertial>` blocks) and can be re-checked in MuJoCo.
Numbers tagged **[ESTIMATE]** or **[EXPECTED]** are *not* verified and are flagged as such
everywhere they appear.

Reference: Camargo J., Ramanathan A., Flanagan W., Young A. (2021). *A comprehensive,

## Part 0 — Answers in one paragraph

Use **our CAD-derived `osl-mujoco/models/osl_v2_bench.xml` as the PRIMARY model** and the
official `OpenSourceLeg_KA_L1` as a **secondary cross-check** built into a matching fixed-base
bench scene. The primary choice is not a quality judgement: the CAD bench is already a bench
(welded base, two joints, no contact, no human, no muscles), its `ctrl` input is already a
reference *angle* so trajectory tracking is the actuator's native semantics, and every
quantity the experiment must log already exists as an authored sensor. `KA_L1` has no bench
scene at all — it exists only composed into a 30-DOF musculoskeletal model with a reflex
controller and ground contact — and its `ctrl` input is motor-shaft torque, so tracking on it
requires writing an outer-loop controller first, which would mix "does the device follow the
trajectory" with "is my controller any good". The torque model is suitable for this
experiment, because the bench never approaches the torque ceiling (peak demand ≈ 33 N·m
against ±142 N·m of authority in *both* models). The mass/inertia difference does **not**
materially affect the comparison: about the knee axis the two models differ by **+3.9 % in
mass and −6.4 % in rotational inertia**, which is far smaller than the servo-gain choice. The
numerical dataset is **not** in the paper — it is on Mendeley Data in three DOI-ed parts, as
MATLAB tables with a **pre-computed 0–100 % gait-phase column**, so **no manual digitization
is necessary**. Recommended single subject: **AB19** (M, 1.70 m, 68.0 kg — the cohort's
median anthropometry), level-ground, self-selected **normal** speed.

There is **one decision I cannot make for you** before code: the bench knee servo is authored
at `kp = 60 N·m/rad` with **no** velocity gain, which will produce roughly **32° of tracking
error at peak swing flexion** for reasons that have nothing to do with the device. See
Part 4 — that is a physics/control assumption and needs your approval.

### 1.2 The quantity that actually decides this experiment

On a **fixed-base bench with no ground contact**, the knee actuator does not fight body
weight. It fights only

```
τ_knee  =  I_knee·α  +  m g d·sin θ  +  b·ω  +  τ_friction
```

so the only mass property that matters is the **knee-distal rotational inertia about the knee
axis**, and the only mass-derived static term is the knee-distal gravity moment. Total device
mass is close to irrelevant here. **[COMPUTED]** from the authored `<inertial>` blocks
(each `diaginertia` rotated by its `iquat`, then parallel-axis shifted to the joint, ankle
held at 0):

| About the **knee** axis | CAD bench (ours) | `KA_L1` (official) | Δ (ours vs theirs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| knee-distal mass | 4.5059 kg | 4.3384 kg | **+3.9 %** |
| COM distance from axis | 0.20028 m | 0.19448 m | +3.0 % |
| **inertia about the axis** | **0.2520 kg·m²** | **0.2692 kg·m²** | **−6.4 %** |
| max gravity moment | 8.853 N·m | 8.277 N·m | +7.0 % |

| About the **ankle** axis | CAD bench (ours) | `KA_L1` (official) | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| ankle-distal (foot) mass | 0.9152 kg | 0.2910 kg | **+214 %** |
| COM distance from axis | 0.02303 m | 0.06904 m | −67 % |
| **inertia about the axis** | **0.00303 kg·m²** | **0.00276 kg·m²** | **+9.7 %** |
| max gravity moment | 0.207 N·m | 0.197 N·m | +4.9 % |

For context, the biological shank + foot this device replaces, for a 68.0 kg / 1.70 m subject
using Winter's anthropometric regressions — **[ESTIMATE], textbook regression, not measured**:
mass ≈ 4.148 kg, COM ≈ 0.2528 m from the knee, inertia about the knee ≈ **0.399 kg·m²**,
gravity moment ≈ 10.29 N·m.

Two things follow, and both are worth reporting:

The **foot-mass discrepancy that is still unresolved as a modelling question is not material
to this experiment.** Our foot is 3.1× heavier than theirs, but its COM sits almost on the
ankle axis (23 mm) while theirs sits 69 mm out, so the ankle-referred inertia and gravity
moment agree to within 10 %. Do not present this as resolving the discrepancy — it does not.
It only means the discrepancy cannot drive the bench result.

Both device models carry roughly **35 % less rotational inertia about the knee than the
biological limb** they replace. The tracking task is therefore not artificially hard, and any
tracking error we report is a statement about control, not about an over-heavy device.

### 1.3 PRIMARY: our CAD-derived `osl_v2_bench.xml`

Seven concrete reasons, in descending order of weight:

It **is already a bench**. Welded base, two hinges, no contact geometry in the active set, no
human, no muscles, 2 kHz fixed timestep, fully deterministic. `KA_L1` has no bench scene; it
exists only inside the composed 30-DOF musculoskeletal model driven by the reflex controller.
Running a knee-tracking experiment there would require either building a new bench scene from
the device fragment (new work) or freezing the human, which changes the physics.

Its `ctrl` **is a reference angle in radians**. `τ = kp·(ctrl − q)` — trajectory tracking is
the actuator's native semantics, so the experiment measures the *device*. On `KA_L1`, `ctrl`
is motor-shaft torque, so we would have to author an outer-loop controller first and the
result would be inseparable from the quality of that controller.

**Every logged quantity the experiment needs already exists as an authored sensor**:
`knee_q`, `knee_qd`, `knee_tau` (actuatorfrc = actual delivered joint torque), plus the ankle
triple, two IMUs, and a 6-axis load cell. Nothing new has to be authored. `KA_L1`'s device
MJCF has no sensors at all.

Its mass and inertia are **CAD-derived from the real OSL V2 assembly**, so the tracking result
is a statement about the actual hardware's inertia — which is the scientific point of a bench
study.

Its **knee ROM contains the whole human reference**, including the small terminal-stance
hyperextension: ours is `[−5°, +120°]`, `KA_L1` is `[0°, +120°]`. A human normal-walking knee
reference that dips 1–3° below zero near terminal stance would be **clipped by a hard joint
stop on `KA_L1`** and would contaminate the tracking error there. Ours would not clip.

It has **explicit joint damping, armature and friction** (0.3 / 0.01 / 0.4 at the knee).
These are provisional placeholders, not hardware values, and must be labelled as such — but a
tracking experiment on `KA_L1`'s frictionless zero-armature ideal hinge would be *less*
physical, not more.

It **does not disturb the MyoAssist integration**, which is mid-flight (the Step 2 rerun is
still outstanding). The two lines of work stay independent.

### 1.4 SECONDARY cross-check: `OpenSourceLeg_KA_L1`

Purpose: answer exactly one question — *how much of the tracking error is attributable to the
device model rather than to the controller?* Build a fixed-base bench scene from
`KA_L1model.xml` plus the two YAML actuators via **mjSpec in Python** (the repo already uses
mjSpec for composition; this avoids adding files to the cloned official repo and avoids the
`meshdir` problem of a hand-written wrapper XML). Then drive it with **the same reference,
the same outer-loop gains, the same timestep, and the same logging**, changing only the device.

Given the 6.4 % inertia difference, the expected outcome is that the two curves nearly
overlap. That is a *useful* result: it converts "our model is different from the official one"
into "the difference is 6 % of the inertia and X° of tracking error", which is a defensible
sentence for your professor. This is **new work and needs your approval** before I write it.

### 1.5 Is the torque model suitable for this experiment?

**Yes — with one framing condition.**

The peak torque demand on the bench, at the fastest part of the human swing phase
(θ ≈ 60°, ω ≈ 6.5 rad/s, α ≈ 93 rad/s² — **[ESTIMATE]** from a 60° flexion excursion in
~0.15 s):

```
I·α        = 0.2520 × 93   = 23.4 N·m
m g d sinθ = 8.853 × 0.866 =  7.7 N·m
b·ω        = 0.3 × 6.5     =  2.0 N·m
friction   =                  0.4 N·m
                             ------
total                        ≈ 33.5 N·m   =  23.5 % of ±142.2 N·m
```

Every idealization that `KA_L1`'s torque model omits — torque-speed curve, current and voltage
limits, drive efficiency, thermal derating, backlash, transmission compliance, device-authored
rotor inertia, actuator bandwidth and latency — bites **near the authority limit or at high
shaft speed**. At 23.5 % of authority and ≤ 7 rad/s they are second-order. So the torque model
is adequate *for this experiment*.

The framing condition: report it as **"torque authority was never the limiting factor in this
experiment"**, not as "the device can do this". The bench result cannot be extrapolated to
stance loading, where the real device operates much closer to its limits. And the required
wording applies verbatim:

> `OpenSourceLeg_KA_L1` models gearbox-related torque authority via knee gain 49.4, ankle gain
> 58.4, and a ±2.88 motor-shaft-torque `ctrlrange`, giving ≈ ±142.27 N·m at the knee and
> ≈ ±168.19 N·m at the ankle. It does **not** model the motor as an electrical system, nor
> current, voltage, torque-speed curve, efficiency, thermal state, backlash, transmission
> compliance, device-authored rotor inertia, or actuator bandwidth/latency. **The model
> represents the available joint torque authority derived from the motor/gear relationship,
> but idealizes the torque production.** The values 49.4 / 58.4 / 30 A / 0.096 N·m/A are
> recoverable model-derivation values from repository history, **not** experimentally verified
> hardware values, unless a hardware datasheet confirms them.

Note our bench actuator's `forcerange` is `±142.2` / `±168.2` — deliberately matched to the
same gear-derived authority, rounded down. So **the two models have effectively the same
torque ceiling** and the ceiling is not what distinguishes them.

### 1.6 The one thing the bench cannot do — state this before your professor asks

A fixed-base bench with no ground contact **cannot reproduce stance kinetics**. The human knee
moment in early stance (≈ 0.4–0.6 N·m/kg, so ≈ 27–41 N·m for a 68 kg subject — **[ESTIMATE]**
from the literature; read the real value off the dataset) is dominated by the body weight
being carried through the limb. On the bench that load does not exist, so the simulated knee
torque in stance will be **near zero while the human knee moment is at its maximum**. That is
an artifact of the boundary condition, not a model error.

Consequence for the design: **the kinematic comparison is valid across the whole cycle; the
torque comparison is only meaningful in swing** (roughly 60–100 % gait cycle, plus pre-swing),
where the human knee moment is small and mostly inertial and gravitational, and where the
human shank+foot (≈ 4.15 kg **[ESTIMATE]**) and the OSL knee-distal mass (4.51 kg
**[MEASURED]**) are within 9 % of each other. Plot the human moment anyway, but shade
0–60 % and label it *not directly comparable — fixed base, no limb loading*.

### 2.2 Best single subject and condition

**Condition: `levelground`, self-selected NORMAL speed.** From §2 — **[MEASURED]** — each
subject walked 5 clockwise + 5 counter-clockwise circuits at each of three self-selected
speeds (slow / normal / fast), 30 circuits per subject. Cohort pelvis velocities were
slow 0.88 ± 0.19, **normal 1.17 ± 0.21**, fast 1.45 ± 0.27 m/s. This matches your professor's
"normal / level-ground walking" exactly.

**Subject: AB19** — M, age 19, **height 1.70 m, mass 68.0 kg** **[MEASURED, Table 1]**. The
cohort is 1.70 ± 0.07 m and 68.3 ± 10.83 kg, so AB19 sits essentially *on* the cohort centre
in both. That matters practically as well as rhetorically: the moments are published
mass-normalized (N·m/kg), so a subject at the cohort mean mass makes the conversion to
absolute N·m transparent and makes the single-subject result maximally representative
**without averaging subjects**, which your professor forbade.

Backups, in order: **AB27** (M, 21, 1.70 m, 68.0 kg — *identical* anthropometry to AB19 and an
independent subject, which makes it the ideal "is this subject-specific?" check), then **AB20**
(F, 21, 1.71 m, 68.0 kg — same anthropometry, different sex).

Note the subject IDs are **not** contiguous: the 22 subjects are AB06–AB21, AB23, AB24, AB25,
AB27, AB28, AB30. There is no AB22, AB26 or AB29 — do not go looking for them.

**Which stride:** §2 says GRF was recorded *"in all locomotion modes during certain
representative steps … and at least one steady-state step"*, and §2.1 says *"Gait cycles
without ground reaction force data were excluded from moment and power analyses."* So on
level-ground most strides have kinematics but only some have moments. Selection rule: filter
to right-leg strides in the **normal**-speed level-ground trials that have **non-null knee
moment**, drop any stride adjacent to a turn or to the start/stop of a circuit, and take a
steady-state straight-line stride from the middle of a circuit. Report the trial file and the
stride index explicitly. **Do not average strides** — but do plot the neighbouring strides
faintly behind the chosen one, so the reader can see the chosen stride is not an outlier.
That satisfies "one subject, one stride" while still showing it is representative.

Fallback if AB19's normal-speed level-ground kinetics coverage turns out to be too thin: the
**treadmill** mode has continuous GRF from the instrumented treadmill (28 speeds, 0.5–1.85 m/s
in 0.05 m/s steps, 30 s each), so pick the treadmill speed nearest AB19's own self-selected
normal speed. Label it clearly as a substitution — your professor asked for level-ground.

### 2.4 How to get one 0–100 % gait cycle

**The dataset already did this for you.** §2.2, verbatim — **[MEASURED]**:

> *"The gait phase of each data point was also calculated using linear interpolation between
> heel strikes from 0 % to 100 % gait cycle. Heel strike was determined from the motion capture
> data as the point of zero linear velocity of the heel marker."*

So there is no event detection to write and no heuristic to defend. Procedure:

1. Load the chosen trial's `ik` (and `id`) table plus the gait-phase / stride information.
2. Select **one right-leg stride** by the rule in §2.2 (normal speed, level-ground, has
   moments, steady-state, mid-circuit).
3. Record the stride's **measured duration** from the heel-strike timestamps. At ~1.17 m/s
   expect ≈ 1.0–1.2 s, i.e. **≈ 200–240 samples at 200 Hz** — **[ESTIMATE]**. This duration
   becomes the sim time base; do not substitute a nominal 1.0 s.
4. Resample to a 0–100 % phase grid at **0.5 % (201 points)** with a monotone interpolant
   (PCHIP) rather than a natural cubic spline, so the resample cannot overshoot at the sharp
   terminal-swing extension.
5. For the 2 kHz sim, interpolate the reference **in time**, and obtain the reference
   *velocity* analytically from the interpolant. Do **not** finite-difference a 201-point
   phase resample and then rescale — that amplifies resampling noise into the derivative term.
6. Repeat the cycle **≥ 5 times** back to back so the servo reaches a steady periodic state.
   Analyse the **last 3 cycles** and discard the transient. Report that you did.

### 2.5 Is manual digitization necessary?

**No, and it would be wrong here.** The numerical data are published, so digitizing is
unnecessary. More importantly, the curves in the paper's figures (Fig. 4 treadmill, Fig. 6
level-ground, Fig. 7 ramp, Fig. 8 stairs) are explicitly **22-subject averages** — §3:
*"reporting the 22 subject average profile for each condition within the mode"* — which is
precisely what your professor told you not to use. Digitizing Fig. 6 would violate the
"one subject, do not average" instruction. Use the repository.

## Part 3 — Recommended experiment design

### 3.1 One-line statement of the experiment

*Drive the knee of the fixed-base CAD-derived OSL V2 bench model with one able-bodied
subject's measured level-ground normal-walking knee-angle trajectory, and quantify how well
the modelled device follows it and at what torque cost, as a function of gait phase.*

### 3.2 Configuration

- Model: `osl-mujoco/models/osl_v2_bench.xml`, **unmodified on disk**.
- Timestep 0.0005 s as authored; control updated every physics step (2 kHz), which is far
  above the ~5 Hz content of the reference.
- Initial state: keyframe `flat`, then a short settle, then the reference starts at 0 % GC.
- Ankle: **held at its keyframe value (0.0418 rad)** for the primary run, so the knee is the
  only driven DOF and the result has one independent variable. A second run driving both
  knee and ankle is a natural follow-up.
- Gravity as authored (−9.81 ẑ). The bench hangs the shank downward from a welded mount, so
  gravity genuinely loads the knee — this is the right boundary condition for the experiment.

### 3.3 How to drive it without touching the model file

The `position` actuator's `ctrl` **is** the reference angle in radians, so the primary loop is
one line: `data.ctrl[knee] = ref(t)`.

The servo gains should be an **explicit, logged experimental parameter**, set at run time on
the compiled model rather than edited into the XML:

```
model.actuator_gainprm[knee, 0] = kp
model.actuator_biasprm[knee, 1] = -kp
model.actuator_biasprm[knee, 2] = -kv        # τ = kp(ref − q) − kv·q̇
```

This keeps `osl-mujoco` byte-identical on disk, makes the gains reproducible from the CSV
header, and lets the as-authored `kp = 60, kv = 0` be *one point in a sweep* rather than a
silent assumption. `forcerange = ±142.2` still clamps the output, so the gear-derived torque
authority is still enforced — which is what we want.

### 3.4 What to log (one row per control step, decimated for the CSV)

`time_s`, `cycle_index`, `gait_phase_pct`, `knee_ref_rad`, `knee_ref_deg`, `knee_sim_rad`,
`knee_sim_deg`, `knee_error_deg`, `knee_ref_vel_rad_s`, `knee_sim_vel_rad_s`,

## Part 4 — Decisions I need from you before writing code

### 4.1 The servo gain — this is the one that will make or break the experiment

The bench knee actuator is authored at **`kp = 60 N·m/rad` with no velocity gain**
**[MEASURED]**. That was a reasonable placeholder for a static model; it is **not** adequate
to track a 1.1 s human knee trajectory. With `τ = kp·(ref − q)` and no feedforward, the
steady-state and dynamic error is `τ_required / kp` — **[COMPUTED]**:

| Operating point (θ, ω, α) | required τ | error at `kp = 60` | error at `kp = 600` |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15°, 1.0 rad/s, 20 rad/s² (early stance) | 8.0 N·m | **7.7°** | 0.8° |
| 35°, 7.0 rad/s, 60 rad/s² (pre-swing) | 22.7 N·m | **21.7°** | 2.2° |
| 60°, 6.5 rad/s, 93 rad/s² (peak swing) | 33.5 N·m | **31.9°** | 3.2° |

Also, the closed-loop natural frequency at `kp = 60` is
`√(kp/I) = √(60/0.252) = 15.4 rad/s = 2.46 Hz` — **below the bandwidth of the reference
itself** (fundamental 0.9 Hz, meaningful content to ~5–6 Hz). And with the authored joint
damping of only 0.3 N·m·s/rad against a critical value of `2√(kp·I) = 7.8`, the servo is
essentially undamped (ζ ≈ 0.04) and will ring.

So: as authored, the experiment would report ~30° of tracking error caused entirely by an
untuned placeholder gain, and any reviewer would say so immediately.

I see two legitimate framings and I recommend running **both**, because together they are a
much stronger result than either alone:

**(A) Tracking-capability framing (the headline).** Choose gains for good tracking and report
them explicitly as a *control choice*, not a device property. A defensible setting is
`kp = 600 N·m/rad` with `kv` set for ζ ≈ 0.7, i.e.
`kv = 2·0.7·√(kp·I) = 2·0.7·√(600 × 0.252) ≈ 17.2`, minus the 0.3 already in the joint, so
`kv ≈ 16.9 N·m·s/rad`. Natural frequency 7.8 Hz, comfortably above the reference. Expected RMS
error a few degrees. This answers your professor's literal request.

**(B) Impedance-realism framing (the second condition).** Hold `kp` at device-plausible
impedance values (the OSL literature uses knee stiffness of order 100–300 N·m/rad in stance)
and report the resulting error honestly. The finding — that a fixed-impedance law at
physically plausible stiffness *cannot* reproduce human knee kinematics without a
phase-scheduled reference — is exactly the known result that motivates finite-state impedance
control on the real OSL. This is a genuinely interesting sentence for your report rather than
a failure.

Concretely I propose a **gain sweep**: `kp ∈ {60 (as authored), 200, 600, 2000}` with matched
ζ = 0.7 `kv` at each, which on a 2-DOF fixed-base model at 2 kHz costs seconds of compute and
turns one number into a curve.

**Because this introduces a control/physics assumption, I am stopping here rather than
choosing for you.** Options, in the order I'd rank them: (i) the sweep above; (ii) `kp = 600`,
`kv ≈ 16.9` only; (iii) leave `kp = 60, kv = 0` exactly as authored and report the large error
as the honest result of the model as it stands.
